What is a BIP39 passphrase, and should I use one?

A passphrase adds a second secret on top of your seed phrase. It can make a found backup useless to a thief. It can also lock you, or your family, out for good.

Short answer

A BIP39 passphrase is an extra word or phrase you choose yourself and add to your seed phrase. Together they open a separate "hidden wallet" that the seed phrase alone cannot reach. Its strength is that someone who finds your seed phrase still can't take the crypto behind the passphrase. Its weakness is that if you forget the passphrase, or write it down wrongly by a single character, that crypto is gone, even with the seed phrase in your hand. For most people the better first step is storing and testing the seed phrase properly. A passphrase is worth adding only once you are confident you can back it up and pass it on as carefully as the words themselves.

It goes by several names: passphrase, BIP39 passphrase, 25th word (or 13th word, with a 12-word phrase), hidden wallet or passphrase wallet. It is not the same as your device PIN or a password for a wallet app.

How a passphrase works

Your seed phrase on its own opens your normal wallet, sometimes called the standard wallet. Add a passphrase and you get a completely different wallet, with different addresses and its own balance. Change the passphrase by one letter and you get yet another wallet.

The key point

There is no such thing as a wrong passphrase. Every passphrase opens a valid wallet. Type yours slightly wrong and you won't see an error, just an empty wallet. That is deliberate, because it stops anyone telling whether a wallet with a passphrase exists, but it also means a typo looks exactly like "nothing here".

Your passphrase is never stored on the device or in the wallet app. You enter it each time you want the hidden wallet, or on some devices you link it to a second PIN.

What it protects against, and what it doesn't

It protects you if:

  • Someone finds your seed phrase. A burglar, a nosy visitor or a family member who comes across your backup gets only the standard wallet, which can be left empty or hold a small amount.
  • Someone forces you to unlock your device. You can open the standard wallet, with a modest balance in it, while the main holdings stay behind the passphrase.

It does not protect you if:

  • You type it into a compromised computer or phone. Malware can record it. Enter it on the device itself wherever possible.
  • The passphrase is stored with the seed phrase. Then anyone who finds one finds both.
  • You forget it, or nobody else knows it exists. No one can recover it for you: not the wallet maker, not support, not KeyCompass.

The risks

A passphrase swaps one risk for another. It reduces the risk of theft from a found backup, and increases the risk of loss.

  • It's a second thing you can't lose. Lose the passphrase and the hidden wallet is gone for good, even with a perfect seed phrase.
  • Small mistakes are fatal. Passphrases are case-sensitive, and a space counts as a character. "Blue Car" and "blue car" open different wallets, and a trailing space opens another.
  • It complicates inheritance. Your family needs the seed phrase, the passphrase, and the knowledge that a passphrase exists at all. An heir who opens the standard wallet and finds it empty may reasonably assume there is nothing there.
  • More complexity, more mistakes. Some self-custody professionals advise most clients not to use one at all, because more people lose funds through over-complicated setups than through theft of a well-stored backup.

Should you use one?

There is no single right answer, but this is a reasonable guide.

Probably not yet if:

  • you haven't yet tested your seed phrase backup, or aren't sure it is stored well
  • you'd struggle to keep a second backup safe, separate and findable
  • your family would need to recover the wallet without help
  • you already use multisig, which removes the single point of failure in a better-structured way

Worth considering if:

  • your seed phrase backup is well stored and tested, and you want protection in case it is found
  • you can store the passphrase separately, as durably, and make sure the right person can find both
  • you are comfortable entering it on the device each time, and testing it regularly

If you are unsure, start without one. You can always add a passphrase later: it creates a new hidden wallet, and you move funds into it once it is tested.

How to set one up safely

  1. Test your seed phrase first. A passphrase is useless on top of a broken backup. See How to test your recovery phrase backup safely.
  2. Choose a passphrase you can write down exactly. Long enough to be hard to guess (several random words is a good approach), but without ambiguous characters or spacing you might misremember. Avoid anything personal, like names or dates.
  3. Write it down before you use it, on paper or metal, exactly as you will type it, including capitals and spaces.
  4. Enter it on the device, not on the computer, wherever your device allows.
  5. Test it before you send anything (see below).
  6. Store the passphrase backup somewhere different from the seed phrase.

On Ledger and Trezor

01LedgerTemporary, or linked to a second PIN

Ledger

Ledger devices offer two ways to use a passphrase, both set up on the device itself:

  • Temporary passphrase. You enter it on the device whenever you want the hidden accounts. It stays active only until the device is switched off.
  • Passphrase linked to a second PIN. You set the passphrase once and choose a second PIN. At start-up, your normal PIN opens your normal accounts and the second PIN opens the hidden ones.

The passphrase can be up to 100 characters, and is case-sensitive. As with your main PIN, three wrong PIN attempts reset the device, so you'll need your seed phrase backup and your passphrase to get back in. Hidden accounts then appear in Ledger Wallet as a separate set of accounts.

02TrezorPassphrase wallets in Trezor Suite

Trezor

In Trezor Suite, passphrase wallets are switched on under Settings → Device → Use passphrase wallets. When you open the wallet, you choose the standard wallet or enter a passphrase to open a passphrase wallet (Trezor calls these hidden wallets).

  • Enter it on the device where you can. Trezor Safe 3, Safe 5, Safe 7 and Model T let you type the passphrase on the device itself.
  • Trezor Suite accepts passphrases of up to 50 characters, and they are case-sensitive.
  • Trezor points out that an empty wallet does not prove you typed the passphrase correctly. It may just be a different, empty wallet.
On a Trezor Model One

The Model One can't take a passphrase on the device, so it is typed on the computer. That is a real weakness if the computer is ever compromised.

Test it before you fund it

This is the step most people skip, and the one that matters most.

  1. Open the passphrase wallet and write down its first receiving address (or the last eight characters of it, and which coin it is for).
  2. Close it and reopen it, typing the passphrase fresh from your written copy, not from memory.
  3. Check the first receiving address matches on the device's screen.
  4. Send a small test amount, then repeat the reopen-and-check once more.
  5. Only then move the rest.

Repeat the reopen-and-check whenever you test your seed phrase, at least once a year.

How to back up a passphrase

Treat the passphrase backup with the same care as the seed phrase, but never in the same place.

  • Written exactly, in capitals or with a note where case or spacing matters.
  • Durable, on metal if your seed phrase is on metal.
  • Separate, so that no single discovery gives someone both.
  • Findable, by the right person, along with a note that a passphrase exists. See Crypto inheritance and family access.

For more on materials and locations, see Seed phrase storage: where should I keep mine?

Mistakes to avoid

  • Typing your real seed phrase or passphrase into a website, including well-known BIP39 tools and "generators". Even a trusted page is still a connected computer.
  • Falling for "seed phrase with balance" offers. Lists and tools claiming to give you seed phrases for funded wallets are scams.
  • Keeping the passphrase with the seed phrase, which defeats the point.
  • Forgetting which wallet holds the funds. Note, somewhere safe, that a passphrase wallet exists.
  • Adding a passphrase in a hurry, for example after a scary headline. A rushed change with an untested backup is far more likely to lose you funds than the threat you were reacting to.

Getting help

Whether a passphrase is right for you depends on how much you hold, who you live with and who would need to recover it. A KeyCompass security review looks at exactly that, along with your backups and succession, without ever seeing your words or your passphrase.
